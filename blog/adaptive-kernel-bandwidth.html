Why Gaussian-kernel bandwidth has a useful middle

A practical intuition behind adaptive spectral bandwidth control.

The problem

Gaussian kernels turn distances into similarities, but the bandwidth σ decides how broadly those similarities spread. Make σ very small and the kernel becomes close to the identity: most points stop seeing one another. Make σ very large and the kernel becomes nearly rank one: everything starts to look the same.

The useful regime

The interesting region is between those extremes. The goal of the adaptive method is to find a local scale where the number of directions visible to the kernel is consistent with the geometric complexity of the underlying neighborhood.

How the paper operationalizes that idea

For each node, the method combines an effective-rank measurement of the kernel matrix with an MST-based intrinsic-dimension estimate and a log-log scaling consistency term. The resulting criterion is minimized over a local bandwidth grid.

Why this matters for learned embeddings

Modern self-supervised encoders can produce high-dimensional embeddings with heterogeneous local geometry. A bandwidth that is reasonable in one region may be too narrow or too broad elsewhere. Local adaptation is therefore not just a parameter tweak; it changes which structure the graph actually resolves.
